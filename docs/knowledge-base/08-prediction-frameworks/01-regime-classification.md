# Regime Classification

> Market regime — broadly, "what kind of market are we in right now?" — is a low-bandwidth, high-leverage feature. Knowing whether you're in a trending, mean-reverting, or chaotic regime determines which strategies have edge. Three approaches: simple thresholding, Hidden Markov Models, and Markov regime-switching econometrics. Each trades complexity for signal quality.

## Definition

A **regime** is a persistent state of the data-generating process. Different regimes have different parameters: trend (mean of returns), volatility, mean-reversion strength, cross-asset correlation. Strategies tuned to one regime typically underperform or lose money in another.

Three canonical approaches, in order of complexity:

### 1. Threshold-based regime classification

Pick a small number of observable variables; define cutoff rules that partition the variable space into regime labels. Examples:

- VIX < 15 → "calm"; 15–25 → "normal"; > 25 → "stressed."
- Price above 200d SMA + rising → "bull"; below + falling → "bear"; otherwise → "chop."
- Realized vol percentile > 80 → "high-vol"; < 20 → "low-vol"; else → "mid-vol."

**Pros**: trivial to implement, interpretable, robust to model misspecification.
**Cons**: discrete jumps at boundaries, no probabilistic uncertainty quantification, no smoothing.

This is the family our `MarketRegimeService` belongs to.

### 2. Hidden Markov Models (HMM)

Assume the observable series is generated by a latent state variable that follows a Markov chain. Standard HMM has K discrete states with state-conditional emission distributions (e.g. Gaussian returns with state-dependent mean and variance). Parameters fit via the Baum-Welch algorithm (forward-backward + EM).

Outputs:
- Posterior probability of each state at each time step.
- Transition matrix `P[next_state | current_state]`.
- Expected duration of each state = `1 / (1 - P[stay])`.

**Pros**: probabilistic outputs, principled handling of state persistence, smoothed predictions.
**Cons**: stationary transition assumption rarely holds in crypto, can overfit with small K, parameter estimation unstable in non-stationary data.

### 3. Hamilton's Markov regime-switching

Hamilton (1989) extended HMM-style regime modeling to econometric time series with auto-regressive dynamics within each regime. The canonical model:

```
y_t = μ_{S_t} + φ_{S_t} × y_{t-1} + σ_{S_t} × ε_t
S_t ~ Markov chain
```

Each regime has its own mean, AR coefficient, and volatility. Estimation typically via maximum likelihood with the Hamilton filter. The original application: US GDP growth into expansion / recession regimes. The framework has since been applied to FX, equity indices, oil prices, and crypto (see Caporale et al. 2018).

**Pros**: integrates regime with time-series dynamics, theoretically principled.
**Cons**: heavy on parameters, sensitive to outliers, often produces "regime" labels that don't survive out-of-sample testing on crypto's non-stationary series.

### 4. Dimensionality-reduction and clustering approaches

Modern variants: cluster a feature vector (vol + skew + correlation + momentum) using K-means or Gaussian mixtures, then label clusters as regimes. Or use a variational autoencoder to learn a continuous "regime embedding" rather than discrete labels.

**Pros**: more flexible, handles continuous regime variation.
**Cons**: cluster labels are unstable across retrains, harder to wire into rule-based downstream systems, opaque.

## When it works

- **As a downstream signal modulator.** Use regime label to adjust thresholds, position sizes, or strategy weights. This is what we do — `SignalEngine.determineSignalLabel` raises the SELL bar in BULL regime, the BUY bar in BEAR regime.
- **As a "stop trading" filter.** Some regimes (CHOP, extreme volatility) have no edge for a strategy. Stopping that strategy in those regimes preserves capital.
- **Persistence is the key property.** Regimes are useful when they last 5–20× the trading horizon. A regime classification that flips daily is noise.
- **Out-of-sample on long-history assets.** Hamilton's framework works well on slow-moving series with 50+ years of data (equity indices, GDP, FX majors). Crypto has too short a history for the parametric versions to estimate reliably.

## When it fails

- **Boundary-crossing artifacts.** Threshold regimes oscillate at the boundary. A single day's close crossing 200d SMA flips the regime label and unflips it the next day. Smoothing (a 2% band, like ours) reduces this; doesn't eliminate it.
- **Non-stationary transition probabilities.** Markov assumption ("the future depends only on the present") fails when regime shifts are caused by exogenous events. The transition probability `P[BEAR → BULL]` after an ETF approval is not the same as before.
- **Look-ahead bias in fitting.** Fitting an HMM on a full history and then claiming the regime probabilities "predicted" regime changes is a common mistake. Always use expanding-window or rolling-window estimation for out-of-sample claims.
- **Label confusion.** A "regime" that's been labeled "BULL" in code may have different meaning from what a strategy developer assumes. Always document the operational definition alongside the label.
- **Sparse regime data.** If a strategy needs to perform across BULL, BEAR, and CHOP, the historical training set must contain enough samples of each. Crypto has had 3 BEAR markets — that's barely enough to characterize one regime type, let alone differentiate within it.

## What we do today (in projectr-x)

`MarketRegimeService.java` implements **threshold-based classification on BTC daily data**. Specifics:

- **Data**: last 60 daily candles of BTCUSDT from `candles` hypertable.
- **Features**: 50-day simple moving average of closes; SMA slope = (current SMA − SMA 7 days ago) / SMA 7 days ago.
- **Band**: `BAND_PCT = 0.02` (2%) around the SMA defines the "no trend" zone.
- **Classification logic** (in `classifyFromBars`):
  - `BULL` if `close > SMA × 1.02` AND SMA is rising.
  - `BEAR` if `close < SMA × 0.98` AND SMA is falling.
  - `CHOP` otherwise (inside band, or close and SMA disagree on direction).
  - `UNKNOWN` if insufficient history (< 50 bars).
- **Refresh**: every 15 minutes via `@Scheduled`. Primes on `StartupEvent` to avoid first-cycle UNKNOWN.
- **Concurrency**: `AtomicReference` for thread-safe regime get/set.

The regime feeds `SignalEngine.determineSignalLabel` as a threshold modulator:
- **CHOP / UNKNOWN**: PR3 transitional thresholds — `strongBuy ≥ 55, buy ≥ 25, strongSell ≤ −40, sell ≤ −15`. SELL side looser than BUY side because the v1–v3 bias-fixes haven't been validated on a full BEAR sample yet.
- **BULL**: SELL threshold reverts to symmetric — `strongSell ≤ −55, sell ≤ −30`. Counter-trend (selling into an uptrend) needs more evidence.
- **BEAR**: BUY threshold tightens — `strongBuy ≥ 70, buy ≥ 40`. Don't catch falling knives.

This is a deliberately simple system. We chose threshold-based over HMM/Hamilton for four reasons:

1. **Interpretability.** A trader can look at the regime label and the BTC chart and confirm or disagree. HMM probabilities are harder to argue with.
2. **Robustness.** No fit parameters means no fitting artifacts. The 50-day SMA, 7-day slope, and 2% band were chosen from domain reasoning, not optimization.
3. **Sample size.** Crypto has too short a history for HMM/Hamilton parameters to be estimated reliably out-of-sample.
4. **Latency to signal.** A daily-bar threshold updates daily. An HMM would also update daily. The complexity of HMM is wasted given the cadence.

### Known limitations and roadmap

- BTC-only regime is applied uniformly across the 13-pair universe. Per-asset regime (or at least per-major) is a natural extension. See `03-altcoin-rotations.md`.
- 2% band edge effects: when BTC sits right around the band boundary, transitions can chatter. Acceptable in practice because the regime is read at 15-minute cadence and small chatter is averaged out by downstream consumers.
- No explicit "transitioning" regime label. A market mid-transition is currently labeled CHOP, which conflates "structurally chopping" with "in the middle of a trend change." A 4-state extension (BULL, BEAR, CHOP, TRANSITIONING) would be a natural refinement once we have post-v4 outcome data to test against.
